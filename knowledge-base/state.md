## Confirmed Findings

- **The "forty feet below, two million pounds are buried" cipher decoding
  has no 19th-century documentary basis; its earliest traceable source is
  a 1949 secondhand/thirdhand account, and an earlier (1894) "decoded"
  rendering used different wording and a different number entirely.**
  The earliest located "decoded" plaintext is an 1894 Oak Island Treasure
  Company prospectus reading "Ten feet below are two million pounds
  buried" — a commercial fundraising document with no named solver or
  method. The now-standard "Forty feet below, two million pounds are
  buried" wording first appears circa 1949 in an unpublished typewritten
  account and an April 1949 letter from Rev. Austen Tremaize Kempton to
  excavator Frederick Blair, who said he got the cipher symbols decades
  earlier from an unnamed schoolteacher, who in turn attributed the
  decoding to an unnamed "Irish instructor." The material only became
  public via Edward Rowe Snow's 1949 book *True Tales of Buried Treasure*.
  The Dalhousie University professor (James Liechti/"Leitchi") now often
  credited with the decoding does not appear in Kempton's own account and
  was in fact Swiss, not Irish — a specific inconsistency two independent
  sources flag as evidence the "professor" attribution was grafted on
  later. — 2026-09-23, `logs/2026-09-23-sq1-provenance-audit.md` §3.
  **Sourcing:** two independently authored, directly-fetched secondary
  sources agreeing in detail — https://ciphermysteries.com/2017/03/19/oak-island-ciphers-part-1-80-foot-rock-cipher
  and http://www.criticalenquiry.org/oakisland/kempton.shtml — corroborated
  by https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Island_mystery's own citations of
  Snow (1949) and the Kempton letter (April 1949). **Disclosed limitation:**
  the 1894 prospectus, Snow's 1949 book, and Kempton's 1949 letter were not
  directly read by this audit; this finding rests on secondary-source
  analysis of those primary documents, not on reading the primary documents
  themselves.

- **The physical stone was not "lost after ~1865" as the project's own
  scaffold assumed — it was documented in continuous, mundane use as a
  bookbinder's leather-beating weight for at least 46 more years, and most
  likely disappeared sometime between 1919 and the early 1930s, not the
  1860s.** The stone reportedly went from a chimney at the Smith homestead
  (~1824) to Creighton's bookbindery in Halifax (~mid-1860s), where a
  firsthand account by Captain H.L. Bowdoin published in *Collier's*
  magazine on 19 August 1911 describes it still in place and in use, its
  inscription being worn away by that very use. Secondary sources (citing
  Harry W. Marshall's 1935 account) say the bookbindery closed in 1919 and
  the stone was left behind; its final disappearance is reported as
  somewhere between "roughly 1912" and "the early 1930s" depending on the
  source, with no confirmed sighting after 1919. — 2026-09-23,
  `logs/2026-09-23-sq1-provenance-audit.md` §2. **Sourcing:**
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Island_mystery and
  https://www.oakislandmystery.com/the-mystery/inscribed-stone, both
  directly fetched. **Disclosed limitation:** rests on these sources' own
  citations (Marshall 1935, a January 1864 Cooke letter, the 1911
  Collier's piece), none of which were independently read by this audit;
  the exact disappearance year is itself disputed between the secondary
  sources consulted.

- **A named, credentialed academic source documents specific pseudoarchaeology concerns with
  *The Curse of Oak Island* (2014–present)**, closing the "search-summary-only" gap the previous
  cycle flagged as insufficient for a Confirmed Finding. Directly fetched (not WebSearch-summary):
  Andre Costopoulos — archaeology professor, University of Alberta —
  `archeothoughts.wordpress.com/2018/12/18/pseudo-archaeology-and-self-correction-in-curse-of-oak-island-fan-communities/`
  (published 2018-12-18, updated 2020-07-09). Costopoulos documents a specific case: the show's
  team, aided by "self-described antiques expert Gabriel Vandervort," identified an iron object as
  a Roman pilum point, in a context where "a previous claim of a roman find on the island, this one
  a sword, had been debunked on the show itself by St-Mary's university materials experts, back in
  2016" — i.e. the show had already had one of its own artifact-identification claims disproven by
  actual university materials scientists, on-camera, and repeated a similar unverified
  identification pattern two years later. This is one specific, named, dated, directly-quoted
  academic critique, not an aggregate reception summary — it does not establish "the" scholarly
  consensus on the show, only that at least one credentialed archaeologist has published a specific,
  documented pseudoarchaeology concern about it.

## Open Questions

- Does any verifiable pre-20th-century primary source establish that the
  90-foot stone inscription and its cipher decoding existed as commonly
  described? **Provisionally answered for the decoding specifically: no —
  see Confirmed Findings above.** Still open for the inscription's
  existence and general appearance, independent of any particular decoding.
- Do the various published transcriptions of the stone's symbols agree
  closely enough to support any cryptanalysis at all? At least two
  disagreeing renderings are confirmed to exist (both memory-based
  reconstructions of a since-lost, since-effaced stone) — see
  `logs/2026-09-23-sq1-provenance-audit.md` §4. Full reconciliation is
  SQ-2's job.
- Is the claimed decoded plaintext reproducible from the best-attested
  transcription under the claimed cipher, independently and without
  researcher-chosen flexibility? See SQ-3. Given the finding above, any
  such test can only assess the internal consistency of a 1949-era claim —
  it cannot be framed as decoding a genuine 19th-century cipher.
- Is the 1795 discovery date and McGinnis/Smith/Vaughan account itself
  reliable, given the earliest print source (1857) predates any named
  details by five years and the full narrative (1862+) is ~67 years
  after the claimed event? Needs a follow-up direct-source session — see
  `logs/2026-09-23-sq1-provenance-audit.md` §1 and "Next steps."
- What is the documented critical/scholarly reception of *The Curse of Oak
  Island* (2014–present) specifically? **Partially answered** — see
  Confirmed Findings above (Costopoulos 2018/2020). One named, credentialed,
  directly-fetched source now exists; this is not yet a survey of "the"
  scholarly reception, and a broader search for additional named academic
  critiques (rather than aggregate WebSearch summaries) remains open.
- Is there a second, physically distinct inscribed stone fragment,
  separate from the 90-foot stone, reportedly found at Smith's Cove in the
  1930s? Surfaced only via `WebSearch` result summaries this session
  (2026-09-25) — wholly unverified, not directly fetched, and not yet
  checked for whether it is itself a TV-show-era ("Curse of Oak Island"
  has run recurring Smith's Cove storylines) claim rather than an
  early-20th-century one. See
  `logs/2026-09-25-sq2-transcription-reconciliation-design.md` §4.
