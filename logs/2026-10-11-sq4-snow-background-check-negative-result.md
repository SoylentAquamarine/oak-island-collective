# SQ-4 — background-checking Edward Rowe Snow (the popularizer), a genuine negative result

**Trigger:** last cycle checked Kempton directly and found a real result (Blair's documented 1949
rebuttal). The other half of the Dare Stones-derived "background check" lead — the Sparkes-style
investigation — points at the other named figure in the chain: Edward Rowe Snow, whose 1949 book *True
Tales of Buried Treasure* is the only reason Kempton's claim ever became public.

## What's already known / not done yet

Already known: Snow's book (1949, p.32) is the first public appearance of the cipher symbols. Not done:
any check on Snow's own reputation or documented track record — whether he has a Dare Stones-style history
of publishing unverified or embellished claims as fact.

## Design and why it's non-circular

Searched specifically for scholarly or historical-society criticism of Snow's accuracy — not his general
biography, which is already on file via Wikipedia's own uncritical summary — then directly fetched
Wikipedia's full article (not just a search snippet) to check for a reception/criticism section before
concluding none exists.

## Honesty precommitment

Report a genuine null result as a null result — not search harder until something turns up, and not treat
"found nothing critical" as equivalent to "confirmed reliable."

## Result

**No documented criticism, scholarly reassessment, or accuracy dispute found for Edward Rowe Snow.**
Multiple searches and a direct fetch of his full Wikipedia article turned up only admiring, biographical
coverage: "a popular storyteller, lecturer, preservationist, and treasure hunter," author of 100+ New
England coastal-history books, credited (without named sourcing in the article itself) with helping save
Fort Warren. His own book titles (*Fantastic Folklore and Fact*, *True Tales of Buried Treasure*) suggest
folkloric framing by Snow's own choice of language, but no source found evaluates whether his specific
claims hold up. No mention of Oak Island, the cipher, or Dungeon Rock in the Wikipedia article at all —
this particular story isn't even one of the ones he's generally remembered for.

**This is a real, disclosed negative result, not a dead end treated as support for anything.** Unlike
Eberhardt in the Dare Stones case (a documented prior history of forged relics, found via one investigative
journalist's actual digging), nothing comparable turned up for Snow in this pass. This could mean: (a) no
such pattern exists, (b) it exists but isn't digitized/indexed in searchable form, or (c) a more targeted
search (contemporary book reviews, New England historical society archives, a Snow biography) would find
it. This project cannot distinguish between those three without deeper, likely non-digital research — named
honestly as the limit of what this pass could check, not glossed over.

## Decision

This closes out the background-check lead from two cycles ago with an honest, two-part result: Kempton
(checked, real finding — Blair's documented rebuttal) and Snow (checked, genuine null result). Neither
confirms nor refutes the broader provenance picture on its own; both are now on file as real, searched,
disclosed data points rather than left as an open "someone should check this" item.
