# SQ-4 — a fourth comparator case: the Horn Papers (1933-1946)

## Precommitment self-review (written before research)

**Known:** SQ-4's panel has three comparator cases (Beale Ciphers, Kensington Runestone, Dare
Stones), with a fourth comparator case named as a standing, not-yet-exercised option in the
Dare Stones log's own closing note. The consolidated checklist (2026-10-11) didn't add a new
case, only synthesized the existing three.

**Not done yet:** no fourth case has been researched.

**Design and why it's non-circular:** search broadly for documented historical-document
fabrication cases (not pre-selecting one that looks favorable to a particular conclusion about
Oak Island), then verify the strongest candidate found against a direct fetch before applying
the same signature-checklist method already used for the first three cases.

**Honesty precommitment:** disclose plainly if this case is a genuine treasure-legend case or a
broader category (SQ-4's scope names "treasure-cipher or treasure-legend cases" specifically),
and disclose exactly which claims are directly-fetched vs. search-synthesis tier.

## Findings

**The case**: the Horn Papers, published 1933-1945 by William Franklin Horn of Topeka, Kansas,
presented as transcriptions of 18th-century family diaries, court records, and maps documenting
early Ohio Valley settlement (1765-1795). **Scope note, disclosed**: this is a genealogical/
frontier-history document fabrication, not a treasure-cipher or treasure-legend case in SQ-4's
own narrowest sense — included here as the broader "historical document fabrication" category
this project's panel already draws from (the Dare Stones case itself was accepted despite being
"structurally different" from Beale/Kensington), with a buried-artifact "corroboration" scheme
(see below) giving it a treasure-hunting-adjacent flavor.

**Directly fetched** (Wikipedia): confirms the core facts — Horn as the credited author/
publisher, the 1933-1945 publication timeline, and that a historical-society committee reported
in *The William and Mary Quarterly* (1946) that the first two volumes were "substantially
hoaxes." A specific detail: in 1936, Horn announced he had dug up lead plates at a location the
papers themselves predicted, which initially appeared to corroborate the documents' authenticity
— a planted/staged "confirming discovery," structurally similar in spirit to how a cipher
claim's own "decoding" can be used to retroactively validate a document's authenticity. Notably,
Wikipedia's own article states there is "no conclusive explanation why W.F. Horn devoted such a
great effort to the forgeries" — motive remains genuinely unresolved even with the forger's
identity settled.

**Search-synthesis tier only, not independently verified this pass**: the specific forensic
signatures reportedly used to expose the fabrication — anachronistic 18th-century phrasing,
the diarists' own apparent ignorance of the Julian calendar (which was in universal period use),
a 19th-century watermark and blue-black ink (not available until 1836) on supposedly
18th-century documents, and chemical "aging" (reportedly via ammonia) — are **not** confirmed by
the directly-fetched Wikipedia article, which gives no forensic detail at all. These signatures
come from the WebSearch synthesis only, tracing to Middleton & Adair's 1947 *William and Mary
Quarterly* article ("The Mystery of the Horn Papers," the field's standard account) — not yet
independently read this cycle.

## Applied against Oak Island's own record

| Horn Papers signature | Oak Island equivalent, already on file |
|---|---|
| Anachronistic language/phrasing (search-synthesis tier only) | **Not yet checkable** — same structural foreclosure as every prior comparator: Oak Island's own stone inscription was never reliably transcribed |
| Physical/chemical dating evidence (ink, watermark, "aging") | **Structurally foreclosed, a real disanalogy** — Oak Island's stone is lost; no physical object survives for this kind of forensic test, the same foreclosure already logged for Kensington and Dare Stones |
| A staged "confirming discovery" (the 1936 lead plates) used to validate the broader claim | **A loose, partial parallel**: Oak Island's own 1894/1949 promotional moments function similarly as later events that lent credibility to the original claim, though neither involves a planted physical artifact the way Horn's lead plates did — a weaker structural match than a direct one |
| Forger's identity settled but motive genuinely unresolved | **A real, useful calibration point, not a direct match**: this is the inverse of Oak Island's own situation (where neither identity nor motive is settled for the anonymous 1949-era chain) — worth naming as a reminder that even a fully "solved" fabrication case can still leave real unanswered questions, so Oak Island's own unresolved status shouldn't be read as itself suspicious |

## Outcome

A fourth comparator case, broadening the panel's own category slightly (historical-document
fabrication generally, not strictly treasure-legend) with full disclosure of that scope
expansion. Its most distinctive contribution isn't a new signature match — most rows repeat the
same structural-foreclosure pattern already established — but the motive-vs-identity
distinction, a genuinely new calibration point not present in any of the first three cases.

## Decision

The consolidated checklist (`data/sq4-fabrication-signature-checklist.md`) should be updated to
include this fourth case, with the scope-expansion caveat stated explicitly rather than quietly
treated as equivalent to the first three.
