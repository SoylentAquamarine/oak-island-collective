## 1. The 1795 discovery story

**What was found:** The earliest located printed mention of any digging on Oak Island is a brief,
unspecific item in the *Liverpool Transcript* (Nova Scotia) of **8 August 1857**, by "J.P. Forks,"
which mentions "very industrious individuals" who "buried large sums" trying to find money
"supposed to have been deposited by the renowned Capt. Kidd" — it does not name McGinnis, does not
mention a stone, and does not give 1795 as a date. The first *detailed* narrative account — naming
Daniel McGinnis and drawing on Anthony Vaughan's memories, and including the tackle-block tree, the
depression, the layered dig, and (per some renderings) the 80–90-foot stone — is J.B. McCully's
account in the *Liverpool Transcript* of **16 October 1862**, roughly 67 years after the claimed
1795 event. A second early account appeared in the Halifax *Colonist* on **2 January 1864**.

**Assessment:** The 1795 date and cast of characters (McGinnis, John Smith, Anthony Vaughan) rest
entirely on oral tradition first committed to print in 1862–1864, not on any contemporaneous
1790s document. Sources disagree even among themselves on details (e.g., whether McGinnis told
"two men" or "two boys," and some later research — cited by one secondary source as "Paul
Wroclawski" — argues the true discovery date may be in the 1780s, not 1795). This matches the
scaffold's framing reasonably well but the scaffold undersold the size of the gap: the earliest
print source (1857) doesn't even mention the year, name, or stone at all; the full story is
entirely an 1862+ retelling.

**Sources directly fetched/read this session:**
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Island_mystery (tertiary, but cites primary newspaper items by name/date)
- Search-result summaries (not directly fetched pages) citing the 1857 Liverpool Transcript and 1862/1864 accounts, via https://www.oakislandmystery.com/the-mystery/early-accounts and https://www.oakislandcompendium.ca/1861-aug-29---liverpool-transcript.html

**Disclosure:** The 1857, 1862, and 1864 newspaper originals themselves were **not** directly read
by this audit — only secondary/tertiary sources (Wikipedia and enthusiast-history sites) that quote
and cite them. This finding should be treated as provisionally well-supported, not independently
verified against the primary newspaper archives.

## 2. The 90-foot stone: discovery, earliest description, physical fate

**Discovery attribution:** Multiple sources credit the **Onslow Company** (active roughly
1802–1804) with recovering the inscribed stone at approximately 80–90 feet depth. Reported
dimensions vary across sources (roughly 2–3 feet long, 12–16 inches wide, dark stone with an
"olive tinge" reportedly unlike local rock).

**Earliest published description:** Located secondary sources point to the McCully 1862 account
and later 1860s correspondence (a "Cooke letter to Hunter-Duvar," dated 27 January 1864, per
Wikipedia's footnote 42) as the earliest surviving descriptions — again decades after the stone's
reported ~1803–04 discovery, and after none of the original diggers apparently left a
contemporaneous written record of it.

**Physical fate — correcting the scaffold's assumption:** The scaffold's background description
said the stone was "possibly built into a Halifax fireplace/bookbindery and lost after ~1865." The
sources located this session give a **more specific and later** trail than "lost after ~1865":
- The stone was reportedly built into a chimney at the Smith homestead on Oak Island around 1824.
- It was later removed and taken to Halifax — to **Creighton's bookbindery**, reportedly brought by
  A.O. Creighton around the mid-1860s — where it was used as a leather-beating weight, its
  inscription reportedly worn away in the process.
- A firsthand account by Captain H.L. Bowdoin, published in **Collier's magazine on 19 August
  1911**, describes seeing the stone still in use at Creighton's bookbindery at that date — i.e.
  the stone was **not** lost by 1865; it was still physically present and documented 46 years
  later.
- One secondary source (Harry W. Marshall, writing in 1935, quoted via Wikipedia) states the
  bookbindery closed in 1919 and the stone was left behind there.
- Sources disagree on the final disappearance date: one location (oakislandmystery.com-linked
  search summary) states the stone "vanishes altogether" by "roughly 1912"; another
  (theoakislandcompendium.com, via search summary) states it disappeared "sometime in the early
  1930's." No source claims a confirmed sighting after 1919.

**Assessment:** The scaffold's "lost after ~1865" framing is not well supported — the best
secondary evidence available says the physical stone survived and was documented in continuous,
mundane use (as a bookbinder's beating stone, its inscription being worn away by that very use)
for at least another 46+ years, disappearing sometime between roughly 1912 and the early 1930s,
most likely around or after the bookbindery's 1919 closure. This is a correction to record.

**Sources directly fetched/read this session:**
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Island_mystery (fetched directly; cites Marshall 1935, Cooke
  1864 letter, Snow 1949 by name/date in its own footnotes, which were not independently verified)
- https://www.oakislandmystery.com/the-mystery/inscribed-stone (fetched directly)
- Search-result summaries only (not fetched as full pages) referencing Collier's 1911 (Bowdoin) and
  theoakislandcompendium.com's "Mystery of the 90 Foot Stone" series

**Disclosure:** This finding rests on Wikipedia's own footnoted citations (Marshall 1935, the 1864
Cooke letter, the 1911 Collier's piece) and other enthusiast-site secondary sources, none of which
were independently read in their original form by this audit. The specific years (1824, mid-1860s,
1911, 1919, disappearance ~1912–1930s) should be treated as reported-by-secondary-source, not
independently confirmed.

## 3. The claimed decoded cipher — the crux question

**This is the most important finding of this audit.**

Two independent, directly-read secondary sources (a Cipher Mysteries analysis and a Critical
Enquiry page specifically devoted to this question) agree on the same reconstruction:

- An **1894** Oak Island Treasure Company prospectus contains the earliest located "decoded"
  rendering of the stone's message: **"Ten feet below are two million pounds buried."** This
  is itself a commercial fundraising document, not independent scholarship, and — per Cipher
  Mysteries — even by 1894 "it seems nobody had decrypted it" in the 1860s, implying the decoding
  (whatever process produced it) happened sometime between 1864 and 1894, already a gap of decades
  from the stone's reported discovery, with no named cryptographer or method given.
- The now-standard **"Forty feet below, two million pounds are buried"** wording — different both
  in specific number (forty vs. ten) and phrasing — **first appears circa 1949**, in an unpublished
  typewritten account and April 1949 letter by **Rev. Austen Tremaize Kempton** to Oak Island
  excavator Frederick Blair. Kempton's letter states he had, decades earlier, commissioned an
  unnamed schoolteacher to write an account of Oak Island; that teacher allegedly supplied the
  cipher symbols and claimed an unnamed "Irish instructor" had deciphered them. Kempton's own
  planned book was never published; his cipher material entered public circulation only because
  **Edward Rowe Snow reproduced it in his 1949 book *True Tales of Buried Treasure***, crediting
  Kempton as his source but giving no further provenance.
- The person "now often said" (per Cipher Mysteries) to have performed the decoding — **James
  Liechti** [spelled "Leitchi" in some retellings], a real Dalhousie University professor of
  languages — is a later attribution not present in Kempton's own 1949 account (which describes an
  anonymous "Irish instructor"); Liechti was in fact Swiss, not Irish, which the Cipher Mysteries
  piece flags as a specific, telling inconsistency suggesting the "Dalhousie professor" identity was
  grafted onto the story after the fact rather than documented at the time.
- Critical Enquiry's dedicated page on this topic (titled, tellingly, "A.T. Kempton's Fake
  Inscription") concludes that because no description of the stone's actual symbols exists prior to
  Kempton's account, and the schoolteacher's identity is unknown and unverifiable, the symbol set
  underlying the "forty feet" decoding should be treated as **fabricated** — in that source's own
  words, "an egregious fraud perpetuated through uncritical repetition across decades of
  literature."

**Bottom line: no 19th-century source documents this decoding at all.** The earliest attested
"decoded" plaintext (1894, "ten feet below") already differs in wording and number from the
now-standard version, has no named solver or method, and cannot be traced to any specific
contemporaneous cryptanalytic work; the specific "forty feet below... two million pounds" wording
and its named-professor attribution trace only to a 1949 secondhand/thirdhand account published
in a 20th-century popular treasure book, not to any earlier documented decoding. This is
independently corroborated by two separate directly-read secondary sources reaching the same
conclusion by different routes, which raises this finding's confidence level within the
constraints of a search-based (not archival) audit.

**Sources directly fetched/read this session:**
- https://ciphermysteries.com/2017/03/19/oak-island-ciphers-part-1-80-foot-rock-cipher (fetched
  and read directly)
- http://www.criticalenquiry.org/oakisland/kempton.shtml (fetched and read directly)
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oak_Island_mystery (fetched and read directly; corroborates via its
  own citation of Snow 1949 and the April 1949 Kempton letter)

**Disclosure:** All three sources are secondary (Wikipedia, a specialist cipher-history blog, an
independent Oak Island critical-research site) analyzing primary documents (the 1894 prospectus,
Snow's 1949 book, Kempton's 1949 letter) that this audit did **not** directly read. However, this
finding is unusually well corroborated for a search-based audit because it is the specific focus
of two independently authored specialist pages that agree in detail (same 1949 date, same Kempton
attribution, same "Irish instructor become Swiss professor" inconsistency), rather than resting on
a single source's framing.

## 4. Multiple disagreeing published renderings of the stone's symbols

**Confirmed to exist.** The oakislandmystery.com page on the inscribed stone (fetched directly)
presents at least two distinct renderings of the symbols — a "general" rendering and Rev. Kempton's
1949 rendering — and explicitly notes that both derive "largely from the memory of an earlier
viewer," not from any systematic tracing, rubbing, or photograph, because (per multiple sources,
including Wikipedia) **no photographs, rubbings, or tracings of the original inscription survive.**
This corroborates the scaffold's claim that disagreeing renderings exist, and adds the important
context that every rendering is a memory-based reconstruction, several steps removed from the lost
original.

**Sources:** https://www.oakislandmystery.com/the-mystery/inscribed-stone (fetched directly)

**Disclosure:** Only two renderings were directly compared this session, both via one secondary
source's presentation of them side by side; a fuller reconciliation (comparing more renderings
symbol-by-symbol) is explicitly SQ-2's job, not this session's.

## 5. Reliability/reception of *Curse of Oak Island* (2014–present) claims

Search results (not all individually fetched as full pages) indicate that professional historians
and archaeologists have publicly characterized the show's evidentiary approach as pseudo-scientific
— one search summary attributes to a Harvard IT-department instructor, described as a long-time
public debunker of Oak Island claims, a characterization of the legend as one of many
"long-running mania for treasure legends all along the eastern seaboard." Another source (Looper)
reports that professional archaeologists who had appeared on the show in earlier seasons have
progressively been "cut ... out of the action" in later seasons. A direct fetch of one candidate
source (Maclean's) failed with an HTTP 403 (paywall/bot-block) and was not substituted with another
full fetch in this session due to time constraints — this claim rests on WebSearch result summaries
only, not a directly-read full article, and should be treated as the weakest-sourced finding in
this log.

**Assessment:** The general pattern — TV-show-era claims held to a lower evidentiary standard than
professional archaeology, and criticized as such by people identifying as historians/archaeologists
— appears real and consistent with the project's own standing trust-tier framework in
`agents/historian.md` and `agents/skeptic.md`, but this specific sub-question needs a proper
follow-up fetch of full, named, dated sources before being cited with confidence in any public-facing
material. Flagged as a gap for a future session, not written up as a Confirmed Finding.
