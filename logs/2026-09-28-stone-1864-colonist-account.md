# Earliest detailed stone description found: The Colonist, 2 January 1864

**Trigger:** ChatGPT's Meeting 13 decision: "Stop broad searches; target named archives and dated
correspondence before 1912." Redirected from generic web search to a specific named compilation of
transcribed early newspaper articles.

## What's already known / not done yet

Already known and read directly: the 1857 *Liverpool Transcript* letters (J.P. Forks) and the 1862
*Liverpool Transcript* McCully account, the latter already giving a brief stone mention ("one at 80 feet
was a stone cut square, two feet long and about a foot thick, with several characters cut on it"). Also
already known, by citation only, never read: a "1864 original" source, name/content unconfirmed. Not
done: reading that 1864 source directly, or finding anything richer than the 1862 account's one-sentence
mention.

## Source and its tier

Found via search: "Early Oak Island Documents, Part 1 of 3 – Early Newspaper Articles," compiled by Les
MacPhie, May 2014, hosted at `oakislandmystery.com`. WebFetch's own extraction failed on the binary (same
compressed-PDF pattern seen elsewhere this session); downloaded the raw file and extracted locally with
`pypdf`. 41 pages, SHA256 `b36fd7b3b9c883f58b4052825ffad064895230ddbb448f08b521527514caccd5`. **Sourcing
tier, disclosed**: this is a named, dated, attributed *transcription* of the original newspapers, not the
raw archival scan image itself (which the project has previously found at `archives.novascotia.ca` but
could not get to render in-session) — one step below a primary scan, but far above an anonymous retelling
or a tertiary narrative site, and internally consistent with the already-verified 1857/1862 primary scans
this project has independently confirmed elsewhere in the same compilation.

## Result

The compilation's third item, **"The Oak Island Folly" (*The Nova Scotian*, 30 September 1861, letter
from "Patrick The Digger," Truro, dated 16 September 1861)**, is a firsthand account from an actual
company digger, describing the pit's physical dig history (wood platforms, flood-tunnel theory, a
non-native grass species used as a evidence marker) in detail — **and mentions no inscribed stone at
all**, even while describing the earlier Onslow-company dig at the same 93–98 foot depth where the stone
is elsewhere reported. This is a real, disclosed absence in a detailed, dated primary account — not proof
the stone doesn't exist, but worth recording honestly as a data point rather than omitting it because it
complicates the narrative.

**The compilation's fourth item is the real find: "History of the Oak Island Enterprise – Chapter I" (*The
Colonist*, 2 January 1864, by "A Member").** This gives, by a wide margin, the most detailed stone
description found by this project to date:

> "Further down was a flag stone about two feet long and one wide, with a number of rudely cut letters and
> figures upon it. They were in hopes this inscription would throw some valuable light on their search,
> but unfortunately they could not decipher it, as it was either too badly cut or did not appear to be in
> their own vernacular. This remarkable stone was pretty far down in the pit, laying in the centre with
> the engraved side down. As it was preserved in the family of Mr. Smith it may be seen by the curious at
> the present day."

Three points of real value beyond the already-known 1862 account:

1. **A period-contemporary judgment on the script's character**: the diggers themselves, in 1864 (or
   describing events from decades earlier — the chapter narrates the original digging, not the 1864
   writing date itself), judged the inscription either badly cut or foreign ("did not appear to be in
   their own vernacular") — the earliest documented interpretive claim about the inscription's nature
   found by this project so far, not a later (post-1900) elaboration.
2. **A primary-tier confirmation of Smith-family custody**, dated 1864 — the Smith/chimney/fireback
   custody chain already extensively logged in this project was previously sourced from later, secondary
   retellings; this pushes a custody claim back to a much earlier, dated primary source, independent of
   those later accounts.
3. **Physical placement detail** not in the 1862 account: "engraved side down," found between tiers of
   charcoal and putty, roughly consistent with (not identical in stated depth to) the 1862 account's "80
   feet" figure — a minor unresolved discrepancy on exact depth worth naming, not resolving here.

## Honest limitations

This is a compiled transcription, not the raw scan — a real, disclosed sourcing-tier limitation. The exact
relationship between "The Colonist" article's 1864 publication date and the underlying events it narrates
(which happened decades earlier) means this is a documented *account*, not necessarily a contemporaneous
*observation* of the stone itself — same caveat that already applies to the existing 1862 account. Not yet
cross-checked against the raw `archives.novascotia.ca` scan for either source.

## Consequence for the project's central question

This does not resolve whether the stone is authentic or what its inscription actually said — it does
push the earliest *documented description with interpretive commentary* back to a dated, named 1864
primary source, materially earlier and richer than what this project had before this cycle, directly
answering the letter (if not yet the full spirit) of the standing "earliest independently dated
description" target.
