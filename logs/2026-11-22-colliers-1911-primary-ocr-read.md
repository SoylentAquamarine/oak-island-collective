## Direct reading from the OCR
The article begins under the heading “Solving the Mystery of Oak Island” at the OCR location corresponding to printed p. 19. On printed p. 20, Bowdoin says his party examined the stone in Halifax in 1909. He describes it as “of a basalt type, hard and fine-grained” and says the characters were “supposed to mean” the familiar “Ten feet below two million pounds lie buried” wording.

In his sixth conclusion, Bowdoin asserts that there never were characters on the rock found in the Money Pit. The OCR around the final sentence of that paragraph is corrupted; do not silently repair it or quote the garbled portion as a verified transcription.

## What this establishes — and what it does not
- **Primary-source reading:** this is Bowdoin's own 1911 account of his 1909 inspection and his argument about the stone.
- It establishes that Bowdoin reported seeing the stone at the Halifax bookbindery and disputed the inscription.
- It does **not** establish that no earlier inscription ever existed, nor does it independently authenticate the stone's reported discovery or earlier history.
- The text was read from Archive.org OCR. The page images were not visually inspected, and the OCR's sixth-conclusion passage is partly defective.
- The 23 February 1912 Amherst Daily News reply attributed to Frederick Blair remains unread. Official reel coverage is not proof that the exact issue survives or contains the reply.

## Research decision
Use this article as the primary source for Bowdoin's own 1909 observations and 1911 position. Do not use it as a substitute for the missing stone or Blair's reply. The next archival task is to obtain and read the exact 23 February 1912 issue, then compare Blair's response with Bowdoin's six conclusions.

No cryptanalysis, image analysis, or new stone transcription was performed.
