# SQ-4 — third Beale-derived signature check: Captain Kidd legend-convention timing vs. Oak Island's 1857 appearance

**Trigger:** the Beale Ciphers comparator case (last cycle) left three of five fabrication signatures
unchecked for Oak Island. This is the one of the three actually answerable with available tooling right
now: vocabulary-dating is blocked by the inscription's own illegibility, and structural-implausibility-
of-the-claimed-plaintext needs a defined test not yet designed. The genre/literary-convention signature —
whether Oak Island's Captain Kidd framing echoes an *already well-established* legend convention rather
than reflecting anything specific to the site — is directly researchable: when did "Captain Kidd buried
treasure here" become a standard, widely-replicated American treasure-legend trope relative to Oak
Island's own earliest confirmed print appearance (the 6 August 1857 *Liverpool Transcript* letter, already
directly read and confirmed by this project — `knowledge-base/state.md` lines ~235-252)?

## What's already known / not done yet

Already known and primary-source-verified by this project: the earliest confirmed print mention of Oak
Island explicitly names Kidd as the pirate who buried the treasure (1857). Not done: any check of when
the general "Kidd buried treasure on the American coast" trope entered circulation, or how widespread it
already was by 1857 — i.e., whether 1857 Oak Island is using an already-standard regional legend
convention (matching Beale's "genre echo" signature) or something more specific to the site.

## Design and why it's non-circular

This is a factual dating question answerable from independently-published folklore/history sources about
Captain Kidd legends in general (not about Oak Island specifically), then compared against Oak Island's
own already-fixed 1857 date. The comparator material is not selected to fit Oak Island's record — it is
whatever the historical record says about Kidd-treasure-legend circulation dates in American folklore,
checked before knowing whether it would support or undercut a genre-echo match.

## Stated prediction

Captain Kidd was executed in 1701, and "Kidd buried treasure" legends are commonly described as having
circulated widely along the American Atlantic coast for well over a century before 1857 (Kidd is the
best-documented "treasure pirate" figure in American folklore, reportedly predating even Poe's 1843
"Gold-Bug," which itself uses a generic pirate-treasure-cipher trope already assumed familiar to readers).
I predict the research will show the Kidd-treasure trope was already a long-standing, widespread
convention well before 1857, supporting a genre-echo match — but I hold this loosely, since I have not
yet verified a specific citation for how early or how widespread it was.

## Honesty precommitment

Report what the sources actually say about dating and spread, including if they contradict this
prediction or if no clear dating can be established.

## Result

**Directly fetched, legendsofamerica.com ("Legends of Captain Kidd's Treasure")**: independently dated
instances of Kidd-treasure digging well before 1857 — "early in the 19th century, the ground was dug
over" near the Piscataqua River (New Hampshire/Maine border), and "as late as 1830, Sergeant Gibbs"
searched for Kidd's treasure on Liberty Island (New York). The page frames the belief's claimed
geographic range as spanning "between Key West, Florida, and Halifax, Nova Scotia" — i.e. already
including the Nova Scotia coast in the general Kidd-treasure belief zone, though without a specific
Oak Island mention or date for when that Nova Scotia extension itself began.

**Directly fetched, Project Gutenberg's full text of Skinner's *Myths and Legends of Our Own Land* (the
actual primary-source compilation, not a summary)**: confirms the same "Key West and Halifax" framing in
Skinner's own words — "he has possibly left some pots of money in holes in the ground between Key West
and Halifax" — in a widely-circulated 19th-century folklore compilation, independent confirmation of the
broad coastal-range convention (though Skinner's book postdates 1857, so this instance alone doesn't
establish pre-1857 circulation by itself).

**Not upgraded**: an initial search-synthesis-tier result claimed Skinner's text states "all buried
treasure tales along the Nova Scotia coast have their ultimate origin in the story of Captain Kidd." The
direct fetch of Skinner's actual text did not contain this specific sentence — it may be from a
secondary summary, a different edition, or a misattribution. Not relied upon; flagged here rather than
silently dropped.

**Not accessed**: folklore.usc.edu's own page (a TLS certificate error blocked the fetch) — a disclosed
tooling limitation, not retried further this cycle.

## Decision

The concrete, independently-dated digging activity (early 1800s at the Piscataqua River; 1830 at Liberty
Island) establishes that American Kidd-treasure-digging was already an active, decades-old folk practice
well before Oak Island's 1857 print appearance — supporting the predicted genre-echo match: Oak Island's
1857 Kidd framing fits into an already well-established regional convention rather than inventing a novel
claim. This is **weaker and more qualified** than the stated prediction: the specific claim that Nova
Scotia's own coastal tales in particular predate or derive from an established pre-1857 Kidd convention
is not directly evidenced here (only the broader "Key West to Halifax" range claim, itself undated as to
origin). Scored as a **real but partial match** — the fourth of five Beale-derived signatures now checked
for Oak Island (one real/partial match newly added here; two from last cycle; two — vocabulary dating and
narrative-structural-implausibility — remain unchecked, vocabulary dating blocked by the inscription's own
illegibility as before).
